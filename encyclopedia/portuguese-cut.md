# portuguese cut

*Cuts and shapes*

**A Portuguese cut is a highly faceted round cut with multiple rows of triangular facets stacked above and below the girdle, giving it far more facets than a standard round brilliant and an intense, lattice-like sparkle. It is a specialty cut, more common in colored gemstones but used for diamonds as well.**

The Portuguese cut is defined by its dense, layered faceting. Where a standard round brilliant has a single main row of facets on the crown and pavilion, a Portuguese cut adds extra rows of triangular and rhombus-shaped facets, typically two or more tiers on each half of the stone. The result is a much higher facet count and a dense, lattice-like pattern of small flashes. A starburst reflection through the table is documented for a modern redesign rather than for the classic pattern: the faceter Jeff Graham, in a design note published by the International Gem Society, writes that his Simple Portuguese, which has one less pavilion tier than the original, shows a starburst on the culet through the table, and that he thinks the old style lacked this. The outline is usually round, though the same faceting scheme is sometimes applied to other shapes.

Face-up, all that faceting produces intense, fine-grained scintillation: many small, busy flashes rather than a few broad ones. The extra rows scatter light in more directions, which is why the style is prized for lively sparkle, especially in transparent colored gemstones, where it is most often seen.

On durability the Portuguese cut behaves like any round: its curved outline has no points or corners to chip, so it is a secure, forgiving shape to set.

The honest practical note is that the Portuguese cut is a labor-intensive specialty. All those extra facets take more time and skill to cut, and the style is far more common in quartz, tourmaline, and other gemstones than in diamond, so as a diamond it is an uncommon, deliberate choice rather than a stock shape. The classic design is also deep, and that depth suits material with a refractive index of about 1.76 or higher; in lower-index material such as quartz it can leave a dead area in the center of the stone, which is why Jeff Graham designed his Simple Portuguese, a variant with one less pavilion tier, for those gems (IGS, Jeff R. Graham, updated 2022-10-31). Diamond, with a refractive index of about 2.42, is well above that threshold. If you want one in diamond, expect limited availability and to seek out a cutter or seller who offers it specifically.

## Sources

- The Portuguese cut is a traditional named cut with several tiers of facets, exceptional scintillation, many facets, considerable labor, and a need for deeper than normal rough. (Source: International Gem Society (IGS, educational nonprofit), "A Guide to Gem Cutting Styles", 2025-07-10. https://www.gemsociety.org/article/gem-cutting-terms/)
- The classic Portuguese design has 161 facets, needs a lot of depth, and performs best in materials with refractive index of about 1.76 or higher; low-RI stones such as quartz show a dead area with the classic angles. (Source: International Gem Society, "How to Cut a Simple Portuguese Gem Design" (Jeff Graham), 2022-10-31. https://www.gemsociety.org/article/simple-portuguese-gem-design/)
- The Portuguese cut is an old, widely used traditional style; with traditional angles it produces a fish-eye in low-refractive-index material. (Source: International Gem Society, "Online Faceting Designs and Diagrams: Gram Portuguese", 2020-12-19. https://www.gemsociety.org/article/online-faceting-designs-diagrams-gram-portuguese/)
- The Portuguese cut works for very deep gemstones with high refractive indices; Graham's redesigned Simple Portuguese drops one pavilion tier and shows a starburst on the culet through the table, which he thinks the old style lacked. (Source: International Gem Society, "Simple Portuguese: Online Faceting Designs and Diagrams", 2019-08-27. https://www.gemsociety.org/article/online-faceting-designs-diagrams-simple-portuguese/)
