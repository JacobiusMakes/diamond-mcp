# Simulant vs synthetic (the distinction)

*Materials and simulants*

**A synthetic diamond (a Lab Grown Diamond) is the same material as a mined diamond, grown in a lab instead of forming in the Earth. A simulant is a different material that merely looks like diamond. In short: a Lab Grown Diamond is a real diamond, a simulant is not.**

This is the single most useful distinction in the whole subject, and it is often blurred. Synthetic and simulant are not synonyms.

A synthetic diamond, better called a Lab Grown Diamond, has the identical chemistry (pure carbon), crystal structure, hardness, and optical properties of a mined diamond. It is grown by high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) or chemical vapor deposition (CVD) methods instead of forming in the Earth, but it is diamond in every physical sense. U.S. regulators make this official. The FTC's 2018 Jewelry Guides define a diamond by its composition and dropped the word natural, so a Lab Grown Diamond is a diamond and must be disclosed as such, never passed off as mined.

A simulant, also called an imitation, is a different material chosen because it resembles diamond to the eye. Cubic zirconia, moissanite, white sapphire, YAG (yttrium aluminum garnet), and even glass are all diamond simulants. They can look similar, but they differ in hardness, density, heat and electrical behavior, and fire, which is exactly how they are identified.

The practical takeaway is simple. Lab Grown Diamond and simulant describe completely different things. A Lab Grown Diamond is a diamond that happens to be grown rather than mined. A simulant is not a diamond at all, whatever it looks like. Good sellers state plainly which one a stone is, and the terms above are the words to look for.

## Sources

- The FTC's revised Jewelry Guides define a diamond by composition and require Lab Grown Diamonds to be clearly disclosed as such, while simulants (imitations) must be disclosed as not being diamonds. (Source: FTC, Guides for the Jewelry, Precious Metals, and Pewter Industries (Federal Register), 2018-08-16. https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2018/08/16/2018-17454/guides-for-the-jewelry-precious-metals-and-pewter-industries)
- 16 CFR 23.12(a) defines a diamond as a mineral consisting essentially of pure carbon crystallized in the isometric system, with no reference to origin. 23.12(c)(3) lists, as a description that is not considered unfair or deceptive, the word cultured for laboratory-created diamonds that have essentially the same optical, physical, and chemical properties as mined diamonds, if the term is qualified by a clear and conspicuous disclosure such as laboratory-created or laboratory-grown conveying that the product is not a mined stone. The note to paragraph (c) refers additional guidance on imitation and laboratory-created diamond representations to 23.25 and 23.27. (Source: 16 CFR 23.12, Definition and misuse of the word diamond (Cornell Legal Information Institute copy of the current CFR), current CFR text (2018 revision); accessed 2026-09-08. https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/23.12)
- In 2018 the FTC removed the word natural from the diamond definition because lab-created diamonds have essentially the same optical, physical and chemical properties as mined diamonds and are therefore diamonds. (Source: U.S. Federal Trade Commission, Statement of Basis and Purpose: Final Revisions to the Jewelry Guides (PDF), 2018-07 (announced 2018-07-24). https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_statements/1393857/g711001_jewelry_guides_statement_of_basis_and_purpose_for_final_jewelry__.pdf)
- 16 CFR 23.25(b) allows a gemstone name to describe a laboratory-grown, laboratory-created, synthetic, imitation or simulated stone only when the name is immediately preceded, with equal conspicuousness, by laboratory-grown, laboratory-created, [manufacturer name]-created or a phrase of like meaning, or by imitation or simulated, so as to disclose clearly that it is not a mined gemstone; 23.25(c) limits laboratory-grown, synthetic and similar terms to products with essentially the same optical, physical and chemical properties as the stone named. (Source: 16 CFR 23.25 (Cornell Legal Information Institute), current CFR text (2018 revision); accessed 2026-09-08. https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/23.25)
